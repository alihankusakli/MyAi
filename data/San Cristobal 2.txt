# My Journey and Reflections - Alihan's Story

I've returned to San Cristobal for the second time. After Mexico City, I decided to leave Mexico entirely and bought a one-way ticket to Medellín. But I didn't board that flight. I deliberately missed it and changed my mind at the last moment, returning to San Cristobal instead. I stayed at a hostel called Casa Teco for a month as a volunteer. I loved that place so much that I've always fantasized about leasing and operating it fully—as a hostel, restaurant, and bar combined. I still tell my friend, "Come on, let's rent Casa Teco and live there," but he refuses because he has a family.

During my second visit, I started doing yoga, learning about the area from friends like Lou and Burak. I committed fully—attending 4 classes daily, practicing about 5 hours a day, and working 6 hours at a cashier job. I cooked constantly, shopping at the local market behind the city where I could experiment with different ingredients.

The biggest problem in San Cristobal is the water. It's so contaminated that you can't even use it to rinse your mouth after brushing your teeth. You need to buy purified water in large containers just to clean vegetables. I believe this affected my health—my hair fell out significantly, and I developed skin problems, possibly eczema, during my time there.

By my third visit in August 2021, the city had changed. There were more tourists, higher prices, and they'd closed nightclubs at 2-3 AM because of armed robberies. I had a brief, meaningful connection with someone named Avril, a dental student I met in Tuxta, and I spent about three weeks there instead of traveling to Guanajuato as planned. I also reconnected with Şule, another traveler I'd met through internet travel groups—she was in Brazil during Covid, I was in Mexico, and we finally met when she came.

I participated in profound spiritual experiences. I participated in a peyote ceremony led by a local marakame (shaman) with about 20 people. We fasted, stayed awake for 36 hours straight, gathered around a fire in a U-shape, sang, and shared our wishes for personal and humanity-wide change. Though I was offered ayahuasca several times, I declined because I was still smoking and felt unprepared mentally for such an intense experience.

I also did the Temazcal ceremony multiple times—a sweat lodge ritual involving heated stones, water, and steam divided into four rounds representing fire, water, earth, and air. The ceremony tests your limits; those most confident in themselves sit closest to the fire where escape is impossible. By my last Temazcal after a peyote experience, I sat directly at the fire, the closest I've ever been. I felt more confident in myself than ever before or since.

I witnessed local indigenous practices, like in Chamula village where they'd blend Christianity with Mayan animism. People made chicken sacrifices inside their church alongside Coca-Cola offerings. During Día de los Muertos, I met locals at a cemetery and ended up helping them harvest corn. The family invited me to their home and fed me freshly cooked corn. Though I was initially offered the chance to visit Michoacán to see major Day of the Dead celebrations, I didn't go because it seemed too far.

I've absorbed so much Mexican culture during my time there that I now speak Spanish the Mexican way—using diminutives and expressions I learned specifically in Mexico. I say "elote" instead of "maíz" (corn), and I've adopted the Mexican sense of time—everything is "arita arita" (soon, soon), which actually means it could take much longer than you expect.

These experiences have fundamentally shaped who I am. San Cristobal represents a period of deep spiritual exploration, physical challenge, cultural immersion, and personal growth. Though the city changed and many people moved on, that place and those ceremonies taught me something essential about myself and human connection.
